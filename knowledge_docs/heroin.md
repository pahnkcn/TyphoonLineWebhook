# Heroin

Source: [NIDA - Heroin](https://nida.nih.gov/research-topics/heroin)

## What is Heroin?

Heroin is an **opioid drug made from morphine**, a natural substance taken from the seed pod of various opium poppy plants grown in Southeast and Southwest Asia, Mexico, and Colombia.

### Forms of Heroin

#### White Powder Heroin
- Fine, white powder
- Usually purer than other forms
- Dissolves easily in water
- Typically snorted or injected

#### Brown Powder Heroin
- Less refined than white heroin
- Darker color due to impurities
- Usually smoked or snorted
- Lower purity

#### Black Tar Heroin
- Dark, sticky substance
- Looks like roofing tar or hard like coal
- Dark color from crude processing
- Usually dissolved and injected or smoked
- Common in western United States

### Street Names
- Smack
- H
- Junk
- Dope
- Horse

## How is Heroin Used?

### Methods of Administration

#### Injecting (Shooting)
- Dissolved in water and injected into veins
- Produces immediate, intense effects
- Highest risk for overdose
- Risk of infectious diseases (HIV, hepatitis)

#### Smoking
- Heated on foil or in pipe
- Vapors inhaled ("chasing the dragon")
- Effects felt within 10-15 minutes
- Lower overdose risk than injecting

#### Snorting (Sniffing)
- Powder inhaled through nose
- Effects felt within 10-15 minutes
- Lower overdose risk than injecting
- Can damage nasal tissues

## How Does Heroin Affect the Brain?

### Immediate Effects
- Rapid conversion to morphine in brain
- Binds to opioid receptors
- Releases surge of dopamine
- Produces intense euphoria ("rush")
- Warm flushing of skin
- Heavy feeling in extremities

### Short-term Effects
- Dry mouth
- Warm flushing
- Heavy feeling in arms and legs
- Nausea and vomiting
- Severe itching
- Clouded mental functioning
- "Nodding" - alternating between wakeful and drowsy states

### Long-term Effects
- Changes in brain structure and function
- Tolerance (needing more to get same effect)
- Physical dependence
- Addiction (heroin use disorder)
- Deterioration of brain's white matter
- Affects decision-making abilities
- Impaired ability to regulate behavior
- Altered responses to stressful situations

## Health Risks

### Physical Health Risks

#### Overdose
- Slowed or stopped breathing
- Decreased oxygen to brain (hypoxia)
- Coma
- Permanent brain damage
- Death
- **Naloxone can reverse overdose**

#### Infectious Diseases
- HIV (from sharing needles)
- Hepatitis B and C
- Bacterial infections of blood vessels
- Heart valve infections (endocarditis)
- Skin infections and abscesses

#### Other Physical Risks
- Collapsed veins
- Constipation and stomach cramping
- Liver and kidney disease
- Lung complications
- Arthritis and rheumatologic problems

### Mental Health Risks
- Depression
- Anxiety
- Antisocial personality disorder
- Increased risk of suicide

### Pregnancy Risks
- Miscarriage
- Premature delivery
- Low birth weight
- Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS)
- Stillbirth

## Heroin Use Disorder (Addiction)

### Signs and Symptoms
- Unable to stop using despite wanting to
- Spending excessive time obtaining, using, recovering
- Neglecting responsibilities
- Continuing use despite negative consequences
- Developing tolerance
- Experiencing withdrawal symptoms

### Withdrawal Symptoms
Withdrawal can begin within hours of last use:
- Restlessness
- Severe muscle and bone pain
- Sleep problems
- Diarrhea and vomiting
- Cold flashes with goose bumps ("cold turkey")
- Uncontrollable leg movements ("kicking the habit")
- Severe heroin cravings

**Withdrawal is not life-threatening for adults, but can be dangerous for fetuses.**

## Heroin Overdose

### Signs of Overdose
- Shallow or stopped breathing
- Blue lips and fingernails
- Clammy skin
- Convulsions
- Coma
- Pinpoint pupils
- Unresponsiveness

### Responding to Overdose
1. **Call 911 immediately**
2. **Administer naloxone (Narcan®)** if available
3. Try to keep person awake
4. Perform rescue breathing if trained
5. Place in recovery position
6. Stay until help arrives

### Naloxone (Narcan®)
- Reverses opioid overdose
- Binds to opioid receptors
- Blocks heroin effects
- Restores breathing
- May require multiple doses for heroin overdose
- Available without prescription in many states
- Training available for family and friends

## Treatment for Heroin Use Disorder

### Medication-Assisted Treatment (MAT)

#### Methadone
- Long-acting opioid agonist
- Taken daily at specialized clinics
- Reduces cravings and withdrawal
- Blocks effects of other opioids
- Used for decades with proven effectiveness

#### Buprenorphine
- Partial opioid agonist
- Available as Suboxone® (with naloxone) or Subutex®
- Prescribed in office settings
- Reduces cravings
- Blocks effects of other opioids
- Lower overdose risk than methadone

#### Naltrexone
- Opioid antagonist
- Blocks opioid effects
- Available as oral (daily) or Vivitrol® (monthly injection)
- Requires complete detoxification before starting
- Non-addictive
- No opioid effects

### Lofexidine
- FDA-approved for opioid withdrawal symptoms
- Non-opioid medication
- Reduces withdrawal discomfort
- Helps during detoxification

### Behavioral Therapies

#### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)
- Helps modify expectations and behaviors
- Develops coping skills
- Addresses triggers

#### Contingency Management
- Voucher-based reinforcement
- Rewards for negative drug tests
- Proven effective

#### Residential and Outpatient Programs
- Various levels of care
- Individual and group counseling
- Support groups
- Aftercare planning

### Treatment Settings

#### Detoxification
- Medically supervised withdrawal
- Usually 5-7 days
- First step, not treatment alone
- Prepares for ongoing treatment

#### Residential Treatment
- 24-hour care
- 30-90 days typically
- Intensive therapy
- Structured environment

#### Outpatient Treatment
- Live at home
- Regular counseling sessions
- Medication management
- Flexible scheduling

## Heroin and Fentanyl

### Dangerous Combination
- Fentanyl increasingly mixed with heroin
- Fentanyl is 50-100 times more potent
- Users often don't know fentanyl is present
- Major cause of overdose deaths
- Fentanyl test strips can detect presence

## Statistics

### Usage
- Heroin use has increased among most demographic groups
- Often starts with prescription opioid misuse
- Shift from prescription opioids to heroin due to cost and availability

### Overdose Deaths
- Heroin-involved overdose deaths remain high
- Many deaths involve multiple substances
- Fentanyl increasingly involved

### Treatment
- Many people with heroin use disorder do not receive treatment
- MAT is underutilized
- Barriers include stigma, cost, and access

## Why Do People Start Using Heroin?

### Common Pathways
1. **Prescription opioid misuse** - Transition from pills to heroin
2. **Curiosity and experimentation**
3. **Peer influence**
4. **Self-medication** - For physical or emotional pain
5. **Economic factors** - Heroin cheaper than prescription opioids

### Risk Factors
- History of prescription opioid use
- Young age (18-25)
- Male gender
- Unemployment
- History of criminal justice involvement
- Co-occurring mental health disorders
- Family history of substance use

## Prevention

### Strategies
- Responsible prescribing of opioids
- Prescription drug monitoring programs
- Education about risks
- Access to treatment
- Naloxone distribution
- Harm reduction services

## Recovery

### Is Recovery Possible?
**Yes!** Many people recover from heroin use disorder with:
- Appropriate treatment
- MAT (medications)
- Behavioral therapies
- Support systems
- Long-term commitment
- Relapse prevention strategies

### Relapse
- Common part of recovery
- Not treatment failure
- Opportunity to adjust treatment
- Learning experience
